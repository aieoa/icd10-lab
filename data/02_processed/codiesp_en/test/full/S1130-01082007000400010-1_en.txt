A 64-year-old man with a personal history of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and recurrent renal colic. He reported diarrhea, with episodes of steatorrhea, constitutional syndrome and epigastric pain lasting two months. The patient was admitted through the emergency department due to jaundice, choluria, and progressive acholia.
On physical examination he showed good general condition, afebrile, normally hydrated and with cutaneous-mucosal jaundice. On abdominal examination, the gallbladder was palpable, with discomfort at that level and no signs of peritonism; the rest of the abdomen was unremarkable. Analytically, he presented hyperbilirubinemia of 17 mg/dl, with an increase in FFAA (2522 U/l) and γGT (107 U/l). No leukocytosis. Viral hepatitis markers were negative and there was an elevated CEA (7.6 ng/dl). The aFP and CA19.9 were normal. Ultrasound showed minimal dilation of the intrahepatic bile duct and discrete common bile duct; The gallbladder was hydropic and without lithiasis. A rounded hypoechoic mass was seen in the head of the pancreas measuring approximately 2 cm, which was not confirmed on the CT scan, which, as additional information, described dilation of the Wirsung duct and absence of lymphadenopathy. Cholangioresonance showed similar findings with clinical suspicion of ampulloma that was not evident in gastroscopy, which was strictly normal. With the diagnosis of obstructive jaundice of probable malignant origin, surgery was performed a week after admission, revealing a mass in the head of the pancreas with a neoplastic appearance, for which cephalic pancreaticoduodenectomy with pyloric preservation and pancreatogastrostomy was performed.
In the pathological study, the surgical specimen showed two clearly differentiated tumor-like areas. One of 1 cm and with well-defined limits in the duodenal wall, and another with indefinite borders of 3 cm in the pancreatic head.

On microscopic examination, the duodenal tumor was made up of cords of uniform, polyhedral cells that infiltrated the muscle fascicles of the wall. They showed nuclei with a low degree of atypicality and very little mitotic activity. The pancreatic tumor was formed by atypical glandular structures that infiltrated the duodenal wall and were lined by columnar cells with very atypical nuclei and a high mitotic index.

Once the diagnosis is oriented. To confirm the different nature of both tumors, immunohistochemical techniques were applied. The resulting immunophenotype allowed us to clearly differentiate both tumors, given that the duodenal tumor was negative for CEA and positive for chromogranin, STH and glucagon and the pancreatic adenocarcinoma was only positive for CEA.
After spending the immediate postoperative period in the ICU, the patient was transferred to the Surgery ward where he recovered favorably and was discharged 19 days after the intervention and transferred to oncology consultation. An adjuvant chemotherapy regimen based on 1 g/m2 gencitabine was administered in four cycles, one of seven weeks and three of three weeks with one rest period. He progressed well in the first few months but later began to experience persistent pain in the right hypochondrium, nausea and vomiting. Given that the radiological examinations are unremarkable, reoperation was performed nine months after the first intervention, confirming the neoplastic recurrence, which was unresectable. The patient suffered a progressive deterioration in his general condition and died 14 months after the first intervention.
