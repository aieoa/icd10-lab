45-year-old male, with no known drug allergies. Among his personal history: ex-smoker (more than 30 pack years), type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, a history of circulating antiphospholipid antibody with a history of two deep vein thromboses. Within his vascular history prior to his admission: in October 2004, a left iliac stent plus a left-right femoro-femoral bypass had been placed. In February 2005, dilation with cryotherapy of the left iliac was performed, which is not effective, so a femoro-popliteal bypass was performed on the first portion of the left lower limb.
In February 2006 he was admitted again to the vascular surgery service due to symptoms of short claudication. In the first intervention, a bilateral ileo-femoral bypass was performed with placement of an epidural catheter for post-surgical analgesia. The postoperative period was not satisfactory, so three days later, a new intervention was performed: common femoral bypass with internal saphenous artery in the left leg. After 24 hours of evolution, he lost the epidural catheter, and intravenous analgesia was prescribed at that time. Four days later the evolution is not adequate, with an ischemic-looking foot, cold toes, signs of prenecrosis, along with intense pain that requires treatment with transdermal fentanyl, rescue boluses of 5 mg of subcutaneous morphine chloride every hour and oral corticosteroids. (prednisone 60 mg/day), suspecting the possibility of small vessel microinfarcts and considering the possibility of amputation of the anterior third of the foot, given the poor situation. A consultation was requested with the anesthesiology service (Pain Unit), which rejected the placement of a new epidural catheter, as the patient was on oral anticoagulant therapy. It was decided to place a sciatic catheter (Stimulong-plus Plexos Catheter set 19 G-100 mm by Pajunk®), which was performed in the operating room, with neurostimulation and through a proximal posterior gluteal route (Labat technique). Perfusion is then started with 0.37% bupivacaine at 3-5 ml/hour. Two days later the foot looks much better. Six days after the catheter was placed, he began to walk, losing the sciatic catheter three days later, and at that time switching to intravenous analgesia. She was discharged 2 days later with a satisfactory evolution.
