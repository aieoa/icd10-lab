We present the case of a 43-year-old man, with no known drug allergies or relevant medical or surgical history, with an abdominal wound from a firearm (pistol with a 9 mm parabellum projectile). The patient is admitted hemodynamically stable to the Emergency Department, with intense abdominal pain. On physical examination, the entry hole of the projectile was observed in the left iliac fossa with exit through the left gluteus, with abdominal pain and signs of peritoneal irritation mainly in the hypogastrium and left iliac fossa. No involvement of the external genitalia or urethrorrhagia was observed. After performing urethro-vesical catheterization, intense macroscopic hematuria was observed. In the admission analysis, only the presence of anemia (Hb: 9.2 g/dl) stands out, with the rest of the biochemical, blood count and coagulation parameters being normal.
Given the patient's hemodynamic stability, it was decided to complete the study with imaging tests prior to surgery. Given the theoretical path of the projectile (once the entry and exit orifices are known), with the suspicion of bladder injury caused by a firearm and given the possible involvement of other organs, it was decided to perform abdomino-pelvic CT and CT-cystography (using bladder refilling by gravity with 300 cc of diluted contrast material).
- Abdominopelvic CT and CT-cystography: extraperitoneal left postero-lateral bladder rupture, with contrast extravasation after filling. Minimal dilation of the left collecting system. Entry wound in the left iliac fossa with areas of hemorrhage in: mesosigma, left lateral aspect of the bladder, left seminal vesicle and left perirectal and ischiorectal areas.

With these findings, exploratory laparotomy was decided. With the patient in the supine position, a supra-infraumbilical midline laparotomy is performed. Initially, we proceed to repair a small perforation in the mesosigma without involvement of the sigmoid loop and to repair a minimal injury at the level of the left common iliac vein, subsequently checking the integrity of the rest of the iliac arterial and venous axis.
Next, an anterior longitudinal median cystotomy was performed and a double bladder perforation was observed, corresponding to the entry holes (4 cm at the bottom of the bladder) and exit (1-2 cm, in the left retromeatrial region) of the projectile. After exploring the integrity of the left distal ureter with a 5F ureteral catheter, it is found that it is completely disinserted in its intramural portion and that the projectile in its path also cuts the left vas deferens. It was decided to perform suture in two planes of the double bladder perforation, after debridement of the devitalized edges and to perform ureteral reimplantation with transvesical technique and double J catheter (26 cm/6 F) in the posterior wall, after releasing the distal ureter and sectioning its end. damaged. After closing, also in double plane, the anterior longitudinal cystotomy, a urethro-vesical catheter and double closed aspiration drainage are left: one at the intraperitoneal level and the other extraperitoneal, separated from the bladder suture line.
Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic therapy was prescribed: Ceftriaxone 2 grams every 24 hours and Metronidazole 1.5 grams every 24 hours, with the initial postoperative period passing favorably. On the 5th day, the patient presented deterioration in general condition, hypotension, fever and leukocytosis with marked left deviation (31% arches). Antibiotic therapy was replaced, empirically, while awaiting the results of the blood cultures, with Imipenem 500 milligrams intravenously every 6 hours, significantly improving the patient both clinically and analytically. An extended-spectrum b-lactamase-producing E. coli, sensitive to Imipenem, grows in blood cultures. A new abdominal-pelvic CT scan was performed, which only showed post-surgical changes, ruling out the existence of fluid collections potentially causing the septic condition.
The rest of the postoperative period passed without incident, initially removing both drains and, on the 10th day, the urethro-vesical catheter. Finally, the patient was discharged after completing 10 days of intravenous antibiotic therapy. It is replaced by Ciprofloxacin 250 milligrams every 12 hours orally, according to previous antibiogram, until the JJ catheter is removed.
One month after the intervention, the patient returned to our service to perform urethro-cystoscopy and removal of the JJ catheter. It is observed that the double bladder wound is completely healed, with the suture of the mucosal plane almost completely reabsorbed. The JJ catheter that guided the ureteroneocystostomy was removed endoscopically without problems. Currently, three months after the intervention, the patient is asymptomatic from a urological point of view.
