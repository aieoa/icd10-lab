A 76-year-old female patient consulted at a hospital Emergency Department due to pain in the mandibular area for three days.
After the medical examination, the patient was sent to her home with the diagnosis of "mandibular osteoarthritis," prescribing painkillers and recommending that she go to the dentist.
Hours later, the patient came to our office, where we applied the study protocol to patients with temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and as a complementary examination we performed an orthopantomography.
Within the patient's personal medical history, it should be noted that she had suffered from high blood pressure for 3 years and hypercholesterolemia for a year.
In the anamnesis about the temporospatial characteristics of the pain, the patient reports that she presents episodes of pain in the bilateral submandibular region, lasting three days, which now radiates to the precordial area and the throat. The pain episodes last between five and ten minutes, with a feeling of shortness of breath and accompanying sweating. On this day she has presented three episodes. This pain has been resistant to the pain medications prescribed in the emergency department.
After carrying out the complete stomatological examination, we found an opening "Click" in the right temporomandibular joint and crepitus on the left side. Occlusally she presented a crossbite and deviation of the dental midline. Mandibular function was preserved with a maximum opening of 39 mm.
The orthopantomography showed signs of mild osteoarthritis in the left temporomandibular joint.
These findings did not justify the patient's pain symptoms, so we issued the presumptive clinical diagnosis of "ischemic heart disease" and referred the patient, urgently, from our consultation to a hospital center for evaluation and treatment.
The patient was admitted to the hospital and while she was undergoing clinical examinations, she again experienced several episodes of jaw pain. The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed signs of growth and systolic overload of the left ventricle, negative T in II, III, aVF and from V4 to V6.
After performing the pertinent complementary studies: blood tests, chest x-ray, echocardiogram and diagnostic catheterization, severe injury was found in two coronary vessels.
She underwent angioplasty and three weeks later she was discharged with the diagnosis of unstable angor (Grade III, B1), severe injury to two coronary vessels and double degenerative aortic injury.
