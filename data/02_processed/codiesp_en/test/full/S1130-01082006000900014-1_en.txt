An 88-year-old woman was admitted for painless obstructive jaundice. His personal history includes deep vein thrombosis, duodenal ulcer with upper gastrointestinal bleeding, and ischemic heart disease under treatment.
She began with symptoms of colicky pain in the right hypochondrium for a week, associated with fever, jaundice (choluria and acholia), nausea and vomiting. On examination, the abdomen was painful in the right hypochondrium.
An abdominal ultrasound was performed, which revealed a homogeneous liver with dilation of the intra-extrahepatic bile duct, a very dilated common bile duct, 22 mm to its most distal portion at its mouth in the papilla. No lithiasis or possible obstructive cause was observed. Pancreatic area with normal ultrasound appearance. Doubtful hypoechogenicity and increased volume of the pancreatic head. Wirsung at the limit of normal and distended gallbladder.
Analytically, the patient presented: total bilirubin 11 mg/dl; 8.23 mg/dl of direct mg/dl, amylase 1,435 mU/ml and hemoglobin 11 g/l.
Transparietohepatic cholangiography was performed as a diagnostic and therapeutic measure. In this case, moderate dilation of the intrahepatic bile duct was observed with moderate dilation of the common bile duct secondary to obstruction at the level of the distal portion of the duodenum. Passage was achieved through the obstruction, leaving an internal-external drainage catheter, confirming adequate placement and function of the catheter through cholangiography.
During the following days, she developed hemobilia with catheter obstruction that was self-limiting, with a drop in hemoglobin levels and no hemodynamic repercussions.
Given the age of the patient and the diagnosis of neoplasia of the head of the pancreas, palliative treatment was chosen with placement of an endoprosthesis (biliary endoprosthesis type wall stent measuring 10 x 70 mm in length) remaining from the proximal common bile duct to the interior of the duodenal light. After its placement, in subsequent days she developed episodes of digestive bleeding with hemodynamic repercussions, which were subsidiary to transfusion. There was no prior diagnostic method because it seemed difficult to suspect the cause.
It was decided to perform selective arteriography to diagnose the location of active bleeding. Selective arteriography of the celiac trunk and hepatic artery was performed, visualizing a 1 cm pseudoaneurysm in the branch of the right hepatic artery. Selective catheterization was attempted to introduce a metallic coil, but this was not technically achieved due to tortuosity of the celiac trunk and hepatic artery that expels the catheter.
A new selective splenic and hepatic arteriography was performed, visualizing permeability of the splenoportal axis and pseudoaneurysm of the intrahepatic branch of the hepatic artery, leading to selective catheterization of the branch with pseudoaneurysm and the introduction of a 3-5 mm diameter metal coil, achieving closure of the bleeding vessel. The evolution was satisfactory with control of bleeding, as well as good drainage of the bile duct with normalization of bilirubin levels.
