A 64-year-old man attended the PC consultation with a dry cough of two months' duration and occasional sensation described by the patient: as if his pylorus was "closed" and he could not eat. His family history included the death of his mother and his father from tuberculosis (TB). His personal history included: poliomyelitis in childhood that left a slight deformity in both lower limbs, duodenal ulcer at the age of 35 without subsequent peptic symptoms, and inguinal herniorrhaphy in 1997. He had been an ex-smoker for 9 years. In the anamnesis he initially reported occasional retrosternal heartburn lasting minutes that subsided spontaneously or with antacids, and also a sensation of gastric fullness. On physical examination he had an asthenic habit. He was afebrile, eupneic and normal colored. Examination of the head, neck, chest, and abdomen was normal. On his first visit, his family doctor requested basic blood tests (hemogram and biochemistry) and chest x-ray (X-ray) and prescribed empirical treatment with omeprazole 20 mg/day. The results of complementary tests were strictly normal and empirical treatment was not effective. Simultaneously, the patient consulted on his own initiative an otorhinolaryngologist who requested an X-ray of the paranasal sinuses where findings compatible with sinusitis were found, which was treated by the specialist with a course of Cefuroxime-axetil 500 mg/12 h (6 days). Subsequently, a month later, given the persistence of the cough and the recent appearance of a slight weight loss, his family doctor requested a consultation with the pulmonologist, performed an extended analysis and a fecal occult blood test, looking for pulmonary pathology and digestive. The pulmonologist performed complementary tests: mantoux, which was positive (12 mm) with Ziehl of sputum and urine repeatedly negative, baseline arterial blood gas, pulmonary function tests (PFP) and provocation test with methacholine, fibrobronchoscopy, computed tomography (CT). thoracic. All these tests did not give pathological findings. Empirical treatment with bronchodilators prescribed by the pulmonologist was also not effective. The new complementary tests performed by the family doctor resulted in a mild normocytic, normochromic anemia (Hb: 11.7 g/dl) with a high erythrocyte distribution index, thrombocytosis, increased globulins, decreased albumin and elevated blood velocity. erythrocyte sedimentation rate: 88 mm/hour. The fecal occult blood study was positive. With this new finding, his family doctor consecutively requested consultation with the gastroenterologist who performed a colonoscopy and an opaque enema in which the only significant finding was colonic diverticulosis, with no other digestive pathology evident. Simultaneously with the follow-up of the digestive condition, 3 weeks after the consultation with the pulmonologist, the patient began to experience asthenia, which is why his family doctor requested consultation with the hospital's Internal Medicine Service. In this service, an abdominal CT is performed in which a large abdominal tumor with probable gastric organ dependence and local involvement is observed, which it is advisable to confirm by gastroscopy and biopsy. When performing oral panendoscopy, they found a benign-looking prepyloric gastric ulcer that was later confirmed by pathological anatomy of a biopsy specimen. In the General and Digestive Surgery Service, an exploratory laparotomy was performed and a large tumor was found, probably retroperitoneal, adhered to the posterior gastric surface at the level of the fundus and multiple extraperitoneal nodules. They removed several of these nodules for biopsy and performed a partial gastrectomy with gastrojejunostomy. The biopsy was reported as an intermediate grade spindle cell sarcoma (grade II) that could be classified as a stromal sarcoma of the gastrointestinal wall. With this diagnosis, he was referred to the Internal Medicine consultations for evaluation of adjuvant treatment, which was rejected. After discharge from the hospital, the patient received home care with palliative treatment from his family doctor until his death, fourteen months after diagnosis.
