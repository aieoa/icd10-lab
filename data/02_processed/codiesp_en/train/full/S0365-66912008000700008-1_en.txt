A 64-year-old male patient was referred to the Ophthalmology emergency room due to double vision and dizziness, with apparently no other notable symptoms; However, instability in standing was observed that could not be justified solely by diplopia.
The only history of interest was that the patient reported having been vaccinated against the flu five days ago.
Visual acuity was 0.7 in both eyes (AO), and both pupils were dilated and areflexic, with no prior contact with mydriatics. He had a limitation in AO abduction, greater in the left eye (LE), in AO supraduction, and difficulty with the rest of the eye movements, reporting horizontal diplopia. The rest of the ophthalmological examination was normal.

Given the suspected diagnosis of Miller syndrome, Fisher was referred to the Neurology service, which detected an ataxic gait, hyporeflexia, and ruled out limb weakness, deciding to admit him for follow-up and complete study. The results of the blood tests and the computed axial tomography (CAT) scan performed in the emergency room were normal.
Twenty-four hours after admission, respiratory distress appeared that required oxygen and physiotherapy, and in the following weeks new neurological symptoms developed: facial paralysis, dysphonia and dysphagia.
During admission, a lumbar puncture was performed, which detected albumin-cytological dissociation, and a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) definitively ruled out space-occupying lesions or demyelination. The electromyographic study did not provide additional information.
The immunological study was positive for anti-GQ-1b antibody, definitively confirming the initial diagnosis.
The patient's symptoms improved two weeks after admission, having received two cycles of immunoglobulins. The clinical evolution was slow and polysymptomatic, with diplopia persisting with prolonged limitation of bilateral abduction.
