A 60-year-old man, with no relevant pathological history, was referred to us from the Urology Service of our Center with the diagnosis of stage IV prostate adenocarcinoma due to multiple metastatic bone involvement and in the hormone-resistance phase.
In February 2004, due to prostate syndrome (frequency and urinary urgency), his Primary Care Physician (PCP) had performed a PSA determination that returned a value of 12 ng/ml, which is why he was referred to a specialist. . Transrectal ultrasound and ultrasound-guided prostate biopsy were performed, which was reported as: bilobular adenocarcinoma, Gleason 7 (4+3). In the extension study (analysis, MRI and bone scintigraphy), retroperitoneal lymph node metastases and multiple foci of pathological hyperuptake (spine, neck and femoral shaft, bone pelvis) were evident. Complete androgen blockade was instituted with gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogue (A-LHRH) and antiandrogen. With this treatment, the PSA level normalized within 4 months (June 04).
The patient received analgesic treatment for bone pain with NSAIDs and morphine. He was also treated with thoracolumbar vertebral irradiation with 30 Gy administered in 10 sessions.
In April 2006, and with the patient asymptomatic, the progressive rise of PSA began to 7.3, 13.1, 21 ng/ml, maintaining testosterone at castration levels. It was decided at this time to discontinue the antiandrogen. The biochemical response was good, achieving normalization in August 06.
Unfortunately, in November 06 he went to the emergency room complaining of intense generalized and disabling bone pain despite the increase in the dose of analgesics (VAS = 8-9) for which he received treatment with Strontium (St) in the Nuclear Medicine Service. Furthermore, the clinical picture is accompanied by PSA levels of 850 ng/ml.
In December 06 we decided, with his informed consent, to start palliative CT with Docetaxel and prednisone. We also institute therapy with bone resorption inhibitors (zoledronic acid). The clinical response was satisfactory with analgesic control (VAS=2-3) and PSA reduction to 150 ng/ml.
On 02-15-07 he went to the emergency room stating that for about 15 days he had been noticing the protrusion of the left eyeball and difficulty in completely separating the eyelids.
Neurological examination
Proptosis, eyelid ptosis, poorly reactive mydriasis secondary to involvement of the third nerve. No orbital murmurs.
Orbital MRI
Metastatic replacement of the entire central segment of the cranial base, the bone marrow of the vertebrae included in the study and part of the cranial diploe. Involvement of the roof and lateral wall of the left orbit that appear expanded and cause proptosis of the eyeball. The left sphenoid greater wing is replaced and thickened and there is clear extension to the wall of the left cavernous sinus and the cleft. Similar changes are observed in the bones that make up the contralateral orbit, but less advanced. Injury to the roof of the left orbit is accompanied by a soft tissue injury that causes inferior displacement of the orbital muscles. In the contrast-enhanced sequences, heterogeneous hyperuptake of all the lesions described is identified.

He received palliative irradiation of the affected areas and showed clear and rapid deterioration, dying on 03-21-07.
