65-year-old patient with a personal history of alcoholism and chronic alcoholic liver disease. He underwent surgery in January 2008 for stenosing sigmoid adenocarcinoma, performing subtotal colectomy and terminal ileostomy in the right iliac fossa. Eight months later, reconstruction of the intestinal transit was performed with end-to-end anastomosis and ileostomy closure. Shortly after (November 2008) the patient was admitted for fecal peritonitis, and a resection of 1.5 meters of small intestine was performed, leaving a definitive terminal ileostomy with an abandoned rectal stump in the pelvis, with no possibility of subsequent reconstruction.
Two months after the second intervention (January 2009) the patient was admitted to the Hospital again due to acute renal failure and secondary hydroelectrolyte alterations, along with ileostomy output greater than 1500 ml/day. At that time he was being treated with Tramadol, Paracetamol, oral iron and a loop diuretic (Furosemide) due to the presence of edema in the lower extremities.
Subsequently, he went to the hospital again on several occasions, being treated in the Emergency Department, with kidney failure and dehydration, along with hydroelectrolyte alterations, which resolved in the department itself and did not require hospital admission.
In July 2009 he went to the emergency room again due to worsening of his general condition, with significant asthenia, prostration, difficulty walking and oliguria. Analysis performed in the emergency room shows: Creatinine: 4 mg/dl (LN 0.6-1.40); Sodium: 113 meq (LN 135-145), Potassium: 6.7 mEq/l (LN 3.5-5), Albumin: 3.1 g/dl (LN 3.4-4); Plasma calcium: 6.7 mg/dl (LN 8.5-10.5); Calcium corrected with albumin: 7.4 mg/dl; Magnesium: 0.5 mg/dl (LN 1.7-2.5); Phosphate: 2.8 mg/dl (LN 2.5-4.5); Venous pH: 7.17 (LN 7.33-7.43); Bicarbonate: 18 mmol/l (LN 22-28); The patient was admitted to Internal Medicine for acute renal failure with hyperlactacidemic metabolic acidosis and electrolyte alterations secondary to losses from ileostomy and diuretic treatment. After several days of parenteral rehydration he was discharged. Then, the diuretic is suspended and treatment is started with oral rehydration salts, Loperamide (2 mg/8 hours) and Magnesium salts (Magnesium Lactate: 500 mg per tablet = 2 mmol = 4 meq of Mg element) at a dose of 8 meq/8 hours/vo).
A week later, the patient suffered syncope at his home with a fall to the floor and mild head trauma to the right frontal region. Upon his arrival to the emergency room, he presented two convulsive episodes of tonic-clonic seizures that subsided with IV diazepam. A few minutes later he suffers a third crisis, which is why treatment with IV Phenytoin is started. and admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU).
A cranial CT scan was performed, revealing minimal subarachnoid hemorrhage in the left suprasylvian region secondary to the CT scan. ECG and chest X-ray without significant alterations.
In the analysis it shows ionic Ca: 2.3 mg/dl (LN 3.9-5.2); 25-hydroxyvitamin D: 10 ng/ml (LN 11-40), Magnesium 0.5 mg/dl; Sodium 125 meq, Potassium 2.8 meq. IV treatment was initiated with fluid therapy for rehydration, as well as IV treatment with magnesium sulfate, calcium gluconate, and potassium chloride to correct electrolyte deficits. During his stay in the ICU he did not present new episodes of seizures or neurological focalities, and upon discharge from this service the levels of magnesium, calcium and potassium had normalized. A consultation with the Nutrition Unit was then requested, revealing in the physical examination a weight of 63 kg (usual weight a year before: 74 kg), height 1.68 m; BMI 22.3 Kg/m2. along with signs of decreased lean mass and fat mass.
During the 3 weeks that the patient was hospitalized, he was treated with dietary measures such as: astringent diet, low in fats and simple sugars, 30-minute rest after meals, and fluid intake outside of meals. In addition, treatment was instituted with oral rehydration salts (Sueroral®), Loperamide (14 mg/day), Codeine (90 mg/day), oral Magnesium supplements (up to 60 meq/day, divided into 4 doses), Carbonate calcium (500 mg) + Cholecalciferol (400 IU) every 8 hours.
The Ileostomy output was 1500-2000 ml/day at the beginning and 800-1000 ml/day at discharge. His nutritional status improved, with nutritional parameters normalizing: cholesterol, albumin, prealbumin and retinol binding protein. The weight was reduced from 63 to 61 kg, and the malleolar and pretibial edema disappeared.
At discharge, Mg levels were below normal, although relatively "safe" (Mg 1.3); with magnesium of 14.7 (LN 50-150), Sodium, Potassium, Total Calcium, Ionic Calcium and Chlorine within normality. In the last days of admission, it was decided to change Cholecalciferol for Calcitriol (0.25 micrograms/day), in order to promote and improve the absorption of magnesium, maintaining the doses of oral calcium (1500 mg/day). PTH levels at discharge were normalized (38pg/ml), as well as vitamin D levels (20ng/ml).
Currently the patient is undergoing check-ups in the Nutrition consultation, presenting good general condition, ileostomy discharges of 1000 + 200 ml/day; with Mg levels within normality (1.9 mg/dl), as well as the rest of electrolytes, having been able to progressively reduce the doses of Magnesium lactate to 9 tablets/day (36 meq/day of Mg element) and the doses of Calcitriol at 0.25 μg/48 hours.
