A 30-year-old male, who one month before admission presented with cough, headache and progressive dyspnea on exertion. Two weeks later the patient presented mucopurulent expectoration and a fever of 38ºC. He went to his doctor who prescribed treatment with amoxicillin and later moxifloxacin and tiotropium bromide. He consulted the emergency room after experiencing dyspnea at rest, intense substernal pain and palpitations.
He was a non-smoker and had a history of bronchial asthma, which he treated with inhaled salbutamol.
On physical examination he had BP: 110/70 mmHg, 37.5º C, he was conscious, tachypneic, sweaty, and did not have jugular engorgement. Pulmonary auscultation revealed diffuse bilateral crackles and cardiac auscultation showed rapid, rhythmic tones without murmurs or extratones. His abdomen was soft and depressible and there were no organomegaly. There was no edema in the extremities and peripheral pulses were preserved.
Regarding laboratory tests, they showed 18,500 leukocytes with left deviation, hemoglobin of 16.3 grams/dl, 474,000 platelets, prothrombin activity of 73%. D-dimer 920 mg/dl. Basal arterial blood gas: pH 7.53, pO2 47 mmHg, pCO2 23 mmHg, CO3H 19.2 mmol/l. Normal biochemistry including troponin and CPK.
The chest x-ray showed severe cardiomegaly and a bilateral interstitial pattern. Given these findings, a transthoracic echocardiogram was performed in which a severe pericardial effusion was observed, with no evidence of tamponade and a left ventricle of normal dimensions.
He was admitted to the intensive care unit, starting treatment with furosemide, levofloxacin, acetylsalicylic acid and non-invasive mechanical ventilation.
In a new echocardiogram, evidence of tamponade was observed, so an evacuative pericardiocentesis was performed, obtaining one liter of serohematic fluid, with the following biochemical characteristics: LDH 2353 U/l, proteins 5.3 g, 9855 cells with a 98 % polymorphonuclear cells.
Seven days later he was transferred to the Internal Medicine Service where the study began to determine the etiology of the pericardial effusion and the interstitial pattern, performing the following tests: antinuclear antibodies, rheumatoid factor, Mantoux, negative. The thyroid profile was normal.
The pericardial fluid culture was negative, as were three blood cultures.
The pathological anatomy of the pericardial fluid was reported to show intense acute inflammation and groups of mesothelial cells with reactive-looking atypia, being negative for malignant cells.
The carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was found to be elevated at 21.53 ng/ml. In addition, serologies were performed against human immunodeficiency virus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, cytomegalovirus, Epstein Barr and Chlamydia pneumonie, which were negative except for IgM to cytomegalovirus, which was positive.
A thoracic CT scan showed two mediastinal lymphadenopathy of pathological size in a pretracheal location of approximately 19 x 15 mm and 18 x 17 mm respectively, and a somewhat larger bilateral pleural effusion on the right side. With pulmonary window, bilateral and diffuse alterations of the lung parenchyma were observed with a septal nodular reticulointerstitial pattern and lobular center with areas of patchy alveolar condensation predominantly distributed in the apical segments of the lower lobes and both upper lobes.

A bronchoscopy was performed, observing upper airways, vocal cords and trachea without alterations, there was a widening in the subcarinal area. The bronchial tree did not present any alterations. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed in the apical segment of the culmen, and the exploration had to be suspended due to poor tolerance, and it was not possible to perform lymphadenopathy puncture or transbronchial biopsy.
The pathological anatomy of the lavage reported the existence of abundant macrophages and epithelial groups with reactive type atypia, being compatible with type II pneumocyte hyperplasia, secondary to acute lung damage.
It was thought that he could present with diffuse interstitial lung disease (DILD) due to the pathological, clinical and radiological characteristics. The first possibility was eosinophilic pneumonia due to the presence of eosinophilia in the peripheral blood, and treatment was started with prednisone 1 mg/kg body weight, with a favorable evolution, and discharged 25 days after admission, without fever or dyspnea.
Seven days later he came again due to a cough with mucous expectoration and later hemoptysis. The chest x-ray showed no changes, so a chest CT was performed, which revealed the ground-glass pattern and a moderate bilateral pleural effusion.
It was decided to perform a lung biopsy to obtain the diagnosis. The report was of diffuse lymphatic permeation (following a nodular interstitial pattern) due to a well-differentiated acinar-papillary adenocarcinoma with abundant psammoma bodies. In the pleural fluid, cells with cytological malignancy characteristics compatible with adenocarcinoma were observed. Histochemical tests were performed confirming the pulmonary origin of the adenocarcinoma.
He is currently undergoing chemotherapy treatment with the combination cisplatin and gemcitabine.
Two cycles have been administered to this point, so we still have no data on the possible response.
