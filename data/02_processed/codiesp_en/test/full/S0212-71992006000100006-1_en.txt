An 18-year-old male patient who went to the emergency department with severe headache and fatigue referred by his primary care physician due to lack of improvement with conventional treatment of upper respiratory viral infections. His personal history only highlights having suffered from infectious mononucleosis. After a catarrhal condition lasting a week, with general malaise, dry cough and fever (up to 38.5ºC), he developed a global headache, more intense frontally, in the last 48 hours, initially without rhinorrhea or nasal obstruction. In isolation, he presented minimal purulent nasal discharge that has sometimes been bloody in recent days. He has not experienced loss of consciousness, seizures, shivering or chills.
When performing the physical examination, we found: Temperature: 37.5 ºC; BP: 137/73 mmHg; P: 71 bpm. Good general appearance with correct hydration and muco-cutaneous perfusion. It doesn't seem affected. Feverish facies. No painful points on sinus percussion. At the skin level, there is a macular and erythematous rash that affects the trunk and neck. No lateral-cervical, axillary or inguinal lymphadenopathy was palpable. Cardiac auscultation is rhythmic, without murmurs. Pulmonary auscultation shows a preserved vesicular murmur, without the presence of rhonchi, crackles or wheezing. The abdomen is soft, depressible, and no masses or megalia are palpable. The upper and lower extremities do not present pathological data, with distal pulses present. The neurological examination is strictly normal, with no evidence of focality or meningism.
The corresponding complementary examinations are carried out, with a rigorously normal chest x-ray, non-pathological urine analysis and a blood count in which leukocytosis stands out with the presence of young forms (calves: 4%), which forces us to consider the differential diagnosis between sinusitis and meningitis; Therefore, a lumbar puncture was performed with 3 predominantly lymphoid cells and 0.3 gr/dl of protein and without glucose consumption. At the same time, a radiographic study of the paranasal sinuses showed no data compatible with sinusitis; Next, to rule out intracranial complications (cavernous sinus thrombosis/encephalitis type), a cranial CT study was performed, which showed occupation of the left sphenoid sinus that is related to acute sinusopathy.

Once the diagnosis of sphenoidal sinusitis was confirmed, it was decided to admit him to hospital to carry out proper monitoring of the patient's evolution and to start intravenous antibiotic treatment with third-generation cephalosporins, a treatment with which the patient progressed correctly, without complications, and the headache and low-grade fever subsided. within 48 hours and being asymptomatic at the time of hospital discharge.
