A 14-year-old male adolescent went to his pediatrician without an appointment due to chest pain in the left anterior hemithorax radiating to the neck. The pain began the day before, began as mild and has worsened, and the patient has observed that the discomfort worsens when lying down and breathing deeply, and improves when he sits and leans his trunk forward. He denies fever or other symptoms, and has no relevant medical-surgical history. The physical examination is completely normal; However, the pediatrician performs an electrocardiogram (ECG) and decides, based on the findings of the test, to refer the patient to the hospital. Before continuing reading, look carefully at the ECG.

Following the systematic reading1, the ECG shows a sinus rhythm at 100 beats per minute. There are no abnormalities in the P wave or in the PR segment, and the QRS complex presents an axis within the left lower quadrant (between 0 and +90o, being positive in I and aVF) without morphological alterations. So far we have not detected any unusual data. When analyzing repolarization, we observed a QTc interval of normal duration, although the presence of negative T waves in leads V3-V6 is striking. We observed a marked depression of the ST segment in V4 and somewhat less in V5, without pathological Q waves or signs of ventricular hypertrophy. Given the patient's symptoms and the electrocardiographic findings, the pediatrician diagnoses acute pericarditis and, with very good judgment, refers the patient to the hospital.
