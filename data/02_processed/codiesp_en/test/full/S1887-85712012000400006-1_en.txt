A 47-year-old woman consulted in the emergency room due to an 8-hour history of continuous and diffuse abdominal pain, along with vomiting, first food-borne and later bilious. The patient's only personal history is having two cesarean sections. In the emergency room, she had a blood pressure of 122/85 mmHg, a heart rate of 91 beats per minute, and an axillary temperature of 35.9 oC. Abdominal examination reveals an infraumbilical median laparotomy scar, the presence of decreased sounds, and diffuse pain on palpation without clear signs of peritoneal irritation. There are no inguinal or femoral hernias.
The blood test revealed only a blood glucose level of 122 mg/dL, a leukocytosis of 15,240 uL, along with neutrophilia of 93%, with the rest of the parameters being normal. Simple abdominal radiography is compatible with small bowel subocclusion and cholelithiasis. Emergency CT scan reveals the presence of dilated loops of small intestine and suggests the possibility of intestinal torsion.

The findings in the complementary examinations, together with the patient's clinical worsening during the observation and study period, increasing vomiting and abdominal pain, as well as signs of peritoneal irritation appearing in the lower abdomen, led to the proposal of an exploratory laparoscopy. . The suspected diagnosis at that time was intestinal obstruction due to flanges, and the time elapsed since admission to the emergency room was 16 hours.
Under general anesthesia, exploratory laparoscopy was performed, identifying a strangulated and necrotic loop of small intestine in the patient's pelvis. This loop occupies the right of Figure 3 (purple/black), which is a direct intraoperative photograph of the blood pressure monitor. the laparoscopic camera. The left part of that image corresponds to the pre-hernial ileum (normal pink color), between both areas of the ileum is the right fallopian tube which is attached to the anterior abdominal wall and the broad ligament extends under it ( not visible in the image) and which would have the hole through which the herniation occurs, in Figure 4 the author represents a schematic drawing to better understand the anatomy of the intraoperative findings. The great dilation of loops of small intestine greatly reduces the work space necessary to manipulate surgical instruments by laparoscopy. This fact, together with the risk of perforation when manipulating the necrotic loop, as well as the impossibility of safely identifying the anatomical structures, requires conversion to open surgery. The reconversion is performed through an iterative infraumbilical laparotomy and it is seen that the cause of the obstruction and strangulation of the ileum is an internal hernia through the right leaf of the broad ligament of the uterus. The hernia is reduced, 25 cm of necrotic ileum is resected, and reconstruction is performed with manual end-to-end anastomosis. Closure is performed on the hernial orifice with continuous 0/0 silk suture. The postoperative period passed normally and the patient was discharged in good clinical condition 11 days after admission. The postoperative control after one month is correct.
