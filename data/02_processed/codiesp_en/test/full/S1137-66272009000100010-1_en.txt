The case of a 30-month-old girl who went to the emergency department due to slight instability in gait and intentional tremor in the last two days is presented. The parents reported that three weeks earlier a mild generalized rash without fever had appeared that resolved spontaneously in a few days. There were no known allergies or previous trauma in her personal history and her vaccination schedule was up to date, including chickenpox. Her psychomotor development followed a normal course. She was re-evaluated 48 hours later, highlighting worsening ataxia that prevented standing, associated with severe irritability and irregular sleep. During the physical examination upon admission, the patient was in good nutritional status and no meningeal signs, external signs of trauma or other pathological findings were found. The neurological examination revealed scanned but intelligible speech appropriate to the context, ataxia with cerebellar characteristics, and intense and generalized intention tremor. Cranial nerves, strength, muscle tone, and deep tendon reflexes were normal and symmetrical.
Upon admission, electroencephalography (EEG) and brain tomography (CT) with iodinated contrast were performed, which did not reveal electroencephalographic or structural abnormalities. Toxins in urine were negative. Lumbar puncture showed mild lymphorrhachia with negative biochemistry and cultures, including Herpes group, neurotropic viruses and Borrelia. Serologies for Herpes simplex virus, Epstein-Barr, Varicella-zoster, enterovirus, Salmonella sp., Streptococcus pyogenes and Borrelia burgdorferi did not provide pathological findings.
On the fifth day after the onset of symptoms, she presented intentional tremor and severe trunk-predominant ataxia that made it difficult for her to sit down. The tremor was aggravated by probable intentional reinforcement myoclonus, and the irritability was extreme. Rapid, saccadic, multidirectional, chaotic but conjugated eye movements then appeared, which could also be seen with the eyes closed, and which decreased when the gaze was fixed. Given the clinical diagnosis of opsoclonus (SOMA), the search for a possible tumor began. An abdominal ultrasound was requested, with normal results, and a simple chest x-ray in which a left parasternal thoracic mass was observed. A thoracic CT scan was performed, which showed a left paravertebral intrathoracic mass, compatible with TCN, confirmed after surgery as localized ganglioneuroblastoma. Neuronal-specific enolase in blood and catecholamines in 24-hour urine were also requested, which were normal.

Opsoclonus decreased after starting high-dose oral prednisone (2 mg/kg/day), and fifteen days after surgery it was no longer observed. The ataxia and irritability improved significantly, but a year later he still required treatment with oral prednisone at minimum daily doses due to relapses of mild gait instability and irritability, with no evidence of tumor recurrence to date. His psychomotor development continues to progress within normality.
