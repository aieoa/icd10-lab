A five-year-old boy was referred by his primary care pediatrician for study due to a right fronto-paretotemporal cranial deformity that had become more evident in recent months. He had had occasional vomiting with bad odors, for a year's duration, and headache accompanied by sonophobia and phonophobia, of variable frequency and mild intensity that had never woken him up at night since he was three and a half years old. The neurological examination was normal (including the fundus), except for macrocephaly with a head circumference of 55 cm (>99th percentile).
The only significant family history is a left frontal AK in the paternal grandfather.
Born from a controlled pregnancy, with normal prenatal ultrasounds and a normal delivery without complications. He had no other personal history of interest. Psychomotor development was normal, with free movement at 13 months, language and sociability appropriate for his age, and he is in school with difficulties in learning to read and write.
In the cranial CT, a large AK is visualized in the right middle cranial fossa with displacement of the midline and collapse of the adjacent ventricular horns, which is confirmed in cranial MRI showing hypointense content in the right fronto-paretotemporal region in T1 and E sequences. hyperintense on T2; which is not modified after contrast with gadolinium.

He was referred to Neurosurgery, where intracranial pressures were monitored with a pneumatic epidural sensor, using a precoronal frontal trephine, finding only the nocturnal pressures pathological. In the face of chronic intracranial hypertension, treatment with cystocysternostomy was decided, communicating the QA with the optic-carotid cistern, using a 30° angled endoscope and a catheter with a double ventriculostomy balloon, to promote the communication of the spaces and the free circulation of the CSF.
