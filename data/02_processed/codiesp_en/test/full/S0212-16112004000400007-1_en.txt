Sixty-three-year-old woman with a personal history of kyphoscoliosis, restrictive respiratory failure, and diverticulosis.
The patient presented peritonitis secondary to perforated diverticulitis, treated with the Hartmann technique. Postoperatively she developed an enterocutaneous fistula, treated with oral enteral nutrition. Given her refusal to undergo surgery, she was discharged with this treatment, when she had a discharge of 200 ml/day, scheduling periodic outpatient check-ups, to which she did not attend.
Three months later, she went to the emergency room due to symptoms of asthenia, anorexia, nausea, dizziness, abdominal pain, a decrease in the output of feces through the colostomy and a significant increase in fistula output, sometimes with a bloody appearance. . She was afebrile, with a BP of 85/50 mmHg and had diffuse pain on abdominal palpation. During her stay in the emergency room, she had a decreased level of consciousness, which improved after the administration of intravenous fluids.
Analytically she presented: hemoglobin 12.3 g/dl; leukocytes 10,400/mm3; blood glucose 102 mg/dl; urea 426 mg/dl; creatinine 2.9 mg/dl; total proteins 9.3 g/dl; Ca 10.5 mg/dl; Na 104 mEq/l; K 6.9 mEq/l; Cl 73 mEq/l; HCO3 17 mmol/l; Urinary Na 6 mEq/l. She was diagnosed with dehydration, acute renal failure, metabolic acidosis, severe hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. Given the suspicion of acute adrenal insufficiency, she was started on treatment with hydrocortisone and continued with IV fluid replacement, with good clinical and analytical response. In the following tests, she showed a decrease in hemoglobin concentration to 7.6 g/dl and received a transfusion of two packed red blood cells.
She was admitted to internal medicine and later transferred to surgery with the diagnosis of intestinal obstruction. On the sixth day after her admission, parenteral nutrition began. The patient was fully conscious, hemodynamically stable, without edema, and renal function, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia had normalized. She weighed 68 kg and was 145 cm tall (BMI 32 kg/m2). The composition of the TPN was: total volume 2,500 ml, 1,500 kcal, N 11 g, glucose 200 g, lipids 40 g, Na 120 mEq, K 60 mEq, Mg 15 mEq, P 15 mEq, Zn 12 mg, Ca 12 mEq . The next day the patient reported a sensation of paresthesia in the hands and perioral areas. Thiamine was administered i.m. empirically. A few hours later she began to present symptoms of decreased level of consciousness and bradypsychia, with preservation of motility in the extremities. The clinical picture progressed during the following day, presenting fluctuating deterioration of the level of consciousness, with aphasia and paraparesis in MSI and becoming stuporous. She was afebrile and her blood pressure was 130/70 mmHg. She discontinued NTP and was maintained on serum therapy and empiric vitamin supplementation. The cranial CT scan was normal. She was transferred to the ICU and, due to suspicion of metabolic encephalopathy, an EEG was performed, which showed non-convulsive status epilepticus, and treatment with IV phenytoin was initiated. Analytically she presented: Hb 10.7 g/l; 12,800 leukocytes with 71.5% granulocytes; blood glucose 91 mg/dl; creatinine 0.5 mg/dl; Na 143 mEq/l; K 2.6 mEq/l; P 0.5 mg/dl; Mg 1.5 mg/dl; CPK 31 IU/l. The ECG did not show the existence of arrhythmias. The arterial blood gas analysis was similar to the previous one.
She was diagnosed with severe hypophosphatemia with hypomagnesemia and hypokalemia. Phosphate salts were administered IV, as well as magnesium and potassium, with good clinical response, progressively improving the neurological situation until normalizing. She was transferred back to surgery and parenteral nutrition was restarted, progressively increasing the caloric intake and with a greater intake of phosphorus, magnesium and potassium. During her admission, she also presented several complications: phenytoin poisoning with neurological symptoms, and sepsis due to Candida spp. related to the central catheter, from which she recovered without sequelae. Oral enteral nutrition and modified oral diet were progressively introduced and TPN and i.v. fluids were discontinued. Given that the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency was not confirmed and there was significant dehydration on admission that could explain the analytical abnormalities, a progressive decrease in corticosteroids was carried out until they were discontinued, without symptoms or signs of adrenal insufficiency appearing. Cortisol after stimulation with ACTH (250 mg) was 43.9 µg/dl at 30 minutes, which reflects a normal adrenal reserve.
The patient rejected intestinal transit reconstruction surgery. The enterocutaneous fistula persisted with an approximate output of 300 cc/day. The patient was discharged with good clinical and analytical status.
