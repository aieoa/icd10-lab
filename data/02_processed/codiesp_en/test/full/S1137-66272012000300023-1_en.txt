A 25-year-old man was admitted in May 2007 for a 48-hour history of non-specific abdominal discomfort accompanied by rectal bleeding the morning he went to the emergency room. His personal history included a slight decrease in coagulation factor VII, two episodes of self-limited lower gastrointestinal bleeding in 1999 (gastroscopy, colonoscopy, scintigraphy with Tc99, enteroscopy, intestinal transit and arteriography without detecting lesions) and another episode in 2004 (gastroscopy). , colonoscopy and ileoscopy and scintigraphy with marked red blood cells without abnormalities) manifested as hematochezia. On physical examination there was nothing remarkable and the patient remained hemodynamically stable. In the emergency tests, he had a normal blood count, biochemistry, and coagulation. Given the patient's history, it was decided to begin the study with a scintigraphy with labeled red blood cells, observing, five hours after administering the tracer, a pathological deposit at the level of the right flank that was compatible with activity in the area of ​​the small intestine at the level of the ileum, cecum or ascending colon. To rule out possible bleeding in these locations, it was decided to perform a colonoscopy that same afternoon in which blood remains were observed along the entire path explored up to 15 cm of the ileum, without finding a clear point of bleeding. Given the situation of persistent lower gastrointestinal bleeding, with the patient remaining stable, it was decided to transfuse 2 packed red blood cells and perform an arteriography in which no anomalies were observed.

Given these findings, it was decided to perform capsule endoscopy enteroscopy (CEE) to evaluate the rest of the small intestine. The same afternoon as the day of the procedure, the patient presented frank rectal bleeding with clinical and analytical repercussions (hemoglobin 4.7g/dl, and hematocrit 13.7%). In this situation, 3 packed red blood cells were transfused and the on-call surgeon was contacted, deciding to perform a new urgent arteriography without evidence of a bleeding point. In this new clinical scenario, an urgent laparotomy was proposed in which Meckel's diverticulum was observed 50 cm from the ileocecal valve with active bleeding from it, without proximal bleeding being observed. Ileal resection was performed including the diverticulum with end-to-end anastomosis with a satisfactory subsequent evolution. The capsule examination visualized the next day revealed the presence of abundant fresh blood remains in the distal sections of the ileum.
