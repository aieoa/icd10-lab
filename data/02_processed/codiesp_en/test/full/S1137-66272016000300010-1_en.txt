A 94-year-old patient, allergic to penicillin and its derivatives, with no relevant medical or surgical history, who does not take regular medication. She reports that in the last 4-5 days she has had episodes of epigastric abdominal pain with nausea and abundant vomiting of bilious characteristics, which is why she went to the emergency room. She has not presented alterations in the depositional rhythm or in the characteristics of the stools. She does not present fever or other associated symptoms.
The physical examination highlights: blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, heart rate 106 bpm, O2 saturation 96% with FiO2 21%, temperature 36.3o C. Conscious. Oriented. Slightly dehydrated. Normal coloration of skin and mucous membranes. Good distal perfusion. Eupneic. Cardiac auscultation: rhythmic heart sounds at 100 bpm, panfocal systolic murmur predominantly in the aortic focus. Lung auscultation: normal vesicular murmur. Abdomen: soft, depressible, not painful on palpation; No masses or visceromegaly were palpable, nor distension or tympanism. Normal peristalsis. Rectal examination: fingertip with traces of normal feces. Lower extremities without edema or signs of deep vein thrombosis.
After the initial assessment, we request:
- Electrocardiogram: sinus rhythm at 105 bpm, without alterations in repolarization, depolarization or conduction disorders.
- Chest x-ray: normal cardiac silhouette without acute pleuroparenchymal alterations.
- Blood analysis: hemoglobin 11 mg/dL with hematocrit 35%. Urea 51 mg/dL with plasma creatinine in normal values.
Medical treatment was started with fluid therapy and antiemetics, and given clinical suspicion of a possible obstructive process of the upper digestive tract due to a motor disorder (gastroparesis), it was decided to perform oral endoscopy.
During oral endoscopy, a solid formation with spherical morphology and yellowish color about 3 centimeters in diameter is identified that is located in the duodenum and causes obstruction of transit at that level. The patient is diagnosed with gallstone ileus due to stones stuck in the duodenum (Bouperet Syndrome). The usual treatment for this entity is surgical, but given the advanced age of the patient, it was decided to attempt endoscopic extraction. First, the duodenum was disimpacted, making a hold using a mesh loop and then the pylorus was crossed in the opposite direction, and the lithiasis was transported to the stomach. Once there, lithotripsy maneuvers were performed without success. Subsequently, with a polypectomy loop, the stone was partially introduced into an overtube, since the diameter of the stone was larger. The stone is removed by endoscopy through the oral cavity. Subsequently, the mucosa of the stomach and duodenum was reviewed without observing erosions or mucosal lesions.

After removal of the stone, the patient's clinical condition resolved and she was asymptomatic. She underwent analytical and radiographic control with no complications observed and she was discharged from the hospital 24 hours later.
