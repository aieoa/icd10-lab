This is a 72-year-old woman with a history of smoking (50 pack-years), alcoholism, and extrinsic asthma who presented with fever, productive cough, and increase in her usual dyspnea. On physical examination she presented low-grade fever (37.9º C), tachypnea and hypoventilation with expiratory wheezing. No visceromegaly or lymphadenopathy was palpable.
The analysis revealed a slight neutrophilia and mild thrombocytopenia and the sputum culture and Gram and Ziehl-Neelsen stains were negative. The chest x-ray showed a left paracardiac condensation, with an air bronchogram, which erased the cardiac silhouette, and the computerized axial tomography (CT) confirmed the existence of a left pulmonary infiltrate, with pleural involvement. With the suspected diagnosis of pneumonia of the lingula, treatment was started with bronchodilators, oxygen therapy and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid with clinical improvement and disappearance of the low-grade fever. The patient was discharged for follow-up in the pulmonology clinic where, given the persistence of the condensation image, the study was continued. Bronchoscopy showed non-specific inflammatory signs in the left bronchial tree with normality in the right; The cytology of the bronchial aspirate and brushing was negative for malignancy. CT-guided fine needle aspiration aspiration (FNAC) revealed monomorphic lymphoid proliferation, suspicious for non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL). A lung biopsy through minithoracotomy confirmed the diagnosis of small B-cell lymphocytic lymphoma (SLLBCL) CD 20 positive, bcl-2 positive, and p-53 negative.

Biological studies showed a slight increase in ESR, b2-microglobulin and the presence of a minimonoclonal IgM lambda band in the proteinogram. Viral serology and autoimmunity study (ANOES) were negative. Abdominal ultrasound showed early signs of diffuse chronic liver disease and abdominal CT showed no lymphadenopathy. A new thoracic CT confirmed the presence of a dense, homogeneous lung mass, measuring 5x7 cm, triangular in shape with a peripheral base and pericardial apex, affecting the lingula and the left upper lobe, accompanied by an atelectatic component with deviation of the mediastinum. The bone biopsy ruled out lymphoma infiltration.
With the diagnosis of low-grade primary pulmonary lymphoma (PPL), oral treatment was started with pulsed chlorambucil, 40 mg orally on day 1, and prednisone, 100 mg/day for 4 days, every 4 weeks. In the first re-evaluation, after four cycles of chemotherapy, the size of the tumor mass had not changed, so the patient was offered treatment with intravenous polychemotherapy, which she rejected. Continuous chlorambucil was then started at a dose of 3 mg/day. A new chest CT showed a decrease in the diameter of the lung mass of more than 50% (8 months later) and more than 75% (16 months after starting daily treatment). As side effects, it presented hematological and hepatic toxicity, both WHO grade I, so the dose of chlorambucil was reduced to 1 mg/day. After two years of continuous treatment, the chest X-ray and CT showed well-ventilated lung parenchymas, with only some residual fibrous tract visible in the left lung. A gallium-67 scintigraphic study showed hyperactivity in the middle third of the left hemithorax, thus confirming the existence of residual disease. Despite this, the patient has maintained therapeutic abstention, in very good partial remission (VPR) and progression-free to date (progression-free interval of 53 months).
