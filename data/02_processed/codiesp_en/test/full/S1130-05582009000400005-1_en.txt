We present the clinical case of a 25-year-old male patient who came to the clinic due to repeated episodes of pain and inflammation around the semi-included lower left third molar. The medical history does not highlight any medical history of interest, the patient does not take any daily medication, except antibiotics and anti-inflammatories, due to the current problem, he is not a smoker and is in perfect health.

After the clinical examination, we requested an orthopantomography as the first diagnostic test. In it we can see how the wisdom tooth in question (38) is in close relationship with the NDI canal, detecting radiological signs determined as clear indicators of high risk of nerve injury during extraction maneuvers. Thus, curvatures are observed in the roots , a darkening around the apices (mesial root) and an erasure of the upper cortex of the canal in contact with the distal root.
Although the orthopantomography showed us a fairly clear image, we decided to request a CT to properly plan the case before deciding on the therapeutic procedure. In the CT images we verify that the contact between the wisdom tooth and the NDI is real and, therefore, the risk of damaging it during extraction is high.

In this situation, we inform the patient of the possibility of performing a coronectomy or partial extraction of the symptomatic third molar. With this technique we aim to eliminate the symptoms caused by pericoronitis, since we achieve direct closure of the wound and the roots are included, preserving the integrity of the NDI.
The patient was informed of the possibility of an infectious complication of pulpal origin, which would force us to reoperate to complete the extraction. Re-entry would also be necessary if, in the long term, root migration occurs, and these again generate symptoms of pericoronitis due to exposure. Only, if this happens, probably the relationship of the roots with the NDI would no longer be so clear, nor would the extraction, therefore, be so compromised.
After obtaining the patient's informed consent, we decided to perform the coronectomy following the following surgical technique: Administration of antibiotic prophylaxis. Making a bayonet incision and raising a full-thickness flap similar to that used for complete extraction of a wisdom tooth. Making a cut of the crown with a fissure bur from the vestibular table, following an angle of approximately 45º. This section is complete so as not to exert force on the roots with the lifters, which requires being very careful when approaching the lingual table so as not to injure the lingual nerve. Subsequently, more dental tissue continues to be removed, with the same fissure bur or with a round bur accessed from the top. The latter is a little easier than with the fissure bur. In this way, the section is at least 3 mm below the bone crests. This is intended to make it easier for the bone to regenerate on the roots and encompass them within the jaw. The exposed root surface should not be treated. Finally, the remains of the follicle are removed, without mobilizing the roots, the remaining tooth, and the wound is sutured with loose stitches that are removed in one or two weeks. We must carry out radiological controls at one month, six months and annually.

All these steps were carried out in our case, and the patient has not presented any complications in one year after performing the intervention.
